\begin{abstract}

Hybrid models that combine physical knowledge with machine learning are attracting growing interest for predicting thermophysical properties.
They often outperform classical models in accuracy while applying to a much wider scope of chemicals and mixtures. 
However, their use in engineering practice remains rare, as they are often hardly accessible through a unified programmatic interface.
To close this gap, we present \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}, a Julia package that provides a variety of state-of-the-art hybrid models for thermophysical property prediction.
The package includes hybrid models for thermodynamic pure-component and mixture properties (e.g., phase equilibria) and transport properties.
All models require only the SMILES of the molecules as input, making them applicable to a broad range of chemical systems.
\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} is built on the \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} package, leveraging its rich thermodynamic solver ecosystem. The included hybrid models significantly improve thermophysical property prediction in various science and engineering applications (e.g., chemical process engineering) and can be coupled with Julia's ecosystem for scientific modeling and simulation. This is demonstrated here with several examples.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge of thermophysical properties of fluids is crucial in many fields of engineering and science, e.g., for developing new chemical and biotechnological processes, optimizing heat pumps, or designing carbon capture and storage technologies. 
However, experimental data on thermophysical properties are notoriously scarce due to the high cost and effort of measurements, making reliable prediction models indispensable. 
In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as an interesting approach to thermodynamic modeling, with hybrid thermodynamic models that combine ML with physical knowledge being particularly promising \cite{hasse_artificial_2026,jirasek_combining_2023}.

Unlike purely data-driven ML models, hybrid models are thermodynamically consistent, i.e., they obey hard physical constraints, and enable robust extrapolation to unseen territory.
The hybridization is achieved either through an appropriate ML model architecture \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1,hoffmann_machine-learned_2025} or by embedding ML models in existing thermodynamic models \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1, hayer_advancing_2025,hoffmann_grappahybrid_2025,hayer_modified_2025}.
Based only on information on the molecular structure, the hybrid models predict thermophysical properties with unprecedented scope and accuracy. 
Among the covered properties are phase equilibria of pure substances and mixtures, which are central to chemical process design, as well as transport properties such as diffusion coefficients \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1,wagner_composition-dependent_2026} that govern molecular mass transfer.

Most hybrid models use a chemical representation method to numerically encode the molecular structure information of the components of interest. Commonly used methods include chemical language models based on Transformer architectures and molecular graph neural networks (GNNs). 
Chemical language models operate on SMILES \cite{weininger_smiles_1988} -- a textual representation of the molecular structure -- and are pretrained on readily available molecular properties.
They output a molecular embedding that can, without retraining, be used as input for hybrid thermodynamic models (the prediction heads).
Examples of chemical language models are ChemBERTa-2 \cite{ahmad_chemberta-2_2022} or SMI-TED \cite{soares_open-source_2025}.
A different approach is used by molecular GNNs \cite{wieder_compact_2020}. Here, the molecular structure is represented as a molecular graph, with atoms as nodes and bonds as edges. The GNN is typically trained with the prediction heads directly on thermophysical properties, thereby encoding the molecular structure into the model's architecture.

Julia already has a rich ecosystem for thermodynamics and chemical engineering. 
\texttt{Clapeyron.jl} \cite{walker_clapeyronjl_2022}, a mature and comprehensive thermodynamic package, is central to this ecosystem. It combines many classical thermodynamic models (including equations of state (EoS) and excess Gibbs energy ($g^{\rm E}$) models) with advanced, efficient solvers. Leveraging Julia's excellent extensibility, several packages are built on \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}, e.g., \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} \cite{schmitt_se-schmittentropyscalingjl_2025} for modeling transport properties, or \href{https://github.com/ClapeyronThermo/Langmuir.jl}{\texttt{Langmuir.jl}} \cite{santana_langmuirjl_nodate} for modeling adsorption.

In this paper, we introduce \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}, a package built on \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} that provides Julia implementations of a variety of state-of-the-art hybrid models, thereby significantly advancing thermophysical property prediction in Julia.
As the models are integrated into \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}, its solvers are directly available, and they can be freely combined with classical models.
This considerably simplifies using hybrid models in practical workflows.
The models are also accessible through the interactive website MLPROP (\url{https://ml-prop.mv.rptu.de/}) \cite{hoffmann_mlprop_2025}, which is aimed at quick calculations for single systems; \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} complements it by allowing the models to be used in automated workflows, embedded in process models, and combined with Julia's wider ecosystem for scientific modeling and simulation.
Additionally, we introduce the subpackage \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl}, which provides implementations of chemical language models. 
An overview of both packages is given in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.

In the following, we briefly present the individual models (Section~\ref{sec:models}) and explain how they are integrated into \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} (Section~\ref{sec:integration}).
We then demonstrate their use with three examples, ranging from phase equilibrium calculations to the dynamic simulation of a chemical process (Section~\ref{sec:examples}).

\section{Models} \label{sec:models}

\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} currently includes GRAPPA \cite{hoffmann_grappahybrid_2025} for pure-component vapor pressures, several hybrid excess Gibbs energy ($g^{\rm E}$) models for liquid mixtures, and two hybrid models for diffusion coefficients (cf.\ Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).
The chemical language models required to generate the input of some of these models are provided by \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl}.
As the package is continuously extended, an up-to-date list of available models is given in the \href{https://se-schmitt.github.io/MLThermoProperties.jl/stable/models}{documentation}.

\subsection{Pure-component vapor pressure model} \label{sec:pure}

GRAPPA is a hybrid model for predicting the vapor pressure of pure components. It combines a GNN with the Antoine equation and was trained on experimental vapor pressure data of about 25,000 components \cite{hoffmann_grappahybrid_2025}. Within GRAPPA, a graph attention network encodes the molecular graph derived from the SMILES of the component of interest, and a prediction head yields the Antoine equation parameters, from which vapor pressures are consistently obtained as a function of temperature. 


\subsection{Excess Gibbs energy models} \label{sec:mix}

The implemented excess Gibbs energy $g^{\rm E}$ models follow the two hybridization strategies outlined above \cite{hasse_artificial_2026}: physical constraints are either hard-coded into the architecture of a flexible neural network, or ML is used to predict the parameters of an established physical model or to improve that model.
These hybrid models provide activity coefficients, from which phase equilibria can be calculated, including vapor-liquid (VLE, in combination with a vapor pressure model like GRAPPA), liquid-liquid (LLE), and solid-liquid equilibria (SLE, in combination with information on the melting temperature and enthalpies of fusion), as well as other excess properties, such as the excess enthalpy $h^{\rm E}$.

HANNA follows the first strategy: it is a hard-constraint neural network that predicts $g^{\rm E}$ from the SMILES of the components, the temperature, and the composition \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}.
Pure-component embeddings from ChemBERTa-2 (see Section~\ref{sec:language}) are processed by a permutation-invariant deep-set architecture that ensures equivariance of the predicted activity coefficients, and the output is corrected to satisfy the pure-component limits; the activity coefficients are then obtained via automatic differentiation and thus obey the Gibbs-Duhem equation by construction.
Two versions are available: \texttt{ogHANNA}, the original model that yields activity coefficients in binary mixtures \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}, and \texttt{HANNA} (alias \texttt{multHANNA}), which also extrapolates to multi-component mixtures by a geometric projection method and was additionally trained on LLE data and excess enthalpies \cite{hoffmann_thermodynamically_2026}. 
Because for HANNA, the neural network is part of the $g^{\rm E}$ model itself, it must be evaluated at every state point of interest, which makes it rather computationally expensive.

UNIFAC 2.0 and modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) 2.0 follow the second strategy: they retain the equations of the group-contribution methods UNIFAC and modified UNIFAC (Dortmund), respectively, and only replace their parameter tables \cite{hayer_advancing_2025,hayer_modified_2025}.
In the hybrid models, a matrix completion method predicts all pair-interaction parameters between the structural groups, including those missing in the published tables. It was trained end-to-end on experimental activity coefficients (and excess enthalpies for modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) 2.0) from the Dortmund Data Bank.
This considerably extends applicability and improves accuracy compared with the original versions.
Because only new parameter tables are required, both models can be readily integrated into process simulators and are implemented directly in \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}.

COGNAC \cite{hoffmann_learning_2026} also follows the second strategy but goes one step further: instead of only completing and improving the UNIFAC's pair-interaction parameters, it also modifies the underlying group-contribution approach by replacing the predefined structural groups with individual atoms whose contributions depend on their molecular neighborhood.
A message-passing neural network encodes each atom together with its molecular neighborhood, from which two prediction heads yield atomic volume and surface-area parameters as well as interaction parameters for every atom pair.
These parameters enter the unchanged modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) equations, so the model naturally generalizes to multi-component mixtures, thermodynamic consistency is guaranteed, and, in contrast to HANNA, the neural network has to be evaluated only once per set of components, making it significantly more computationally efficient.
COGNAC was trained on about 820,000 experimental data points comprising VLE, LLE, SLE, activity coefficients at infinite dilution, and excess enthalpies of binary mixtures.

\subsection{Transport property models} \label{sec:transport}

Currently, two models for transport properties are available in \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}.
Both are models for diffusion coefficients based on the Stokes-Einstein (SE) equation, whose prediction is multiplied by a positive scaling factor obtained from a neural network ensemble that takes molecular descriptors derived from the SMILES as input.
As the scaling factor does not depend on temperature, the physically consistent temperature dependence of the SE equation is retained.
Besides SMILES, both models require only pure-component viscosities as input, which, if not experimentally available, can be obtained, e.g., from entropy scaling models in \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} \cite{schmitt_entropy_2024}.

ESE (Enhanced Stokes-Einstein) predicts diffusion coefficients $D_{ij}^\infty$ of a solute $i$ at infinite dilution in a pure solvent $j$ \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1}; the required viscosity is that of the solvent at the temperature of interest.
Choosing the solute as solvent ($j=i$) yields the self-diffusion coefficient of the pure component.

HADES is the successor of ESE and extends the concept to composition-dependent self-diffusion coefficients in liquid mixtures with any number of components, covering the entire range from infinite dilution to the pure components \cite{wagner_composition-dependent_2026}.
The descriptors of the solvent components are aggregated by a deep-set model, which ensures invariance to permutation, and the viscosity of the mixed solvent is obtained by logarithmic mixing of the pure-component viscosities.
HADES was trained on 2,526 experimental data points for 600 systems.

\subsection{Chemical language models} \label{sec:language}

Some hybrid models, e.g., HANNA, do not process SMILES directly but require a numerical molecular embedding as input, i.e., a fixed-size numerical vector that encodes information about the molecular structure.
These embeddings can be generated from SMILES by the chemical language models provided by \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl}, where two language models are available: ChemBERTa-2 \cite{ahmad_chemberta-2_2022}, in the variant whose embeddings serve as input for HANNA \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}, and SMI-TED \cite{soares_open-source_2025}, both pretrained on tens of millions of molecules from PubChem.
SMI-TED additionally provides a decoder that converts an embedding back into a SMILES.
Both language models are implemented natively in Julia based on \texttt{Lux.jl} \cite{pal_lux_2026}.
When HANNA is used within \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}, the embeddings are generated internally, so that only the components need to be specified (see Section~\ref{sec:ex_mix}).
As \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl} is a registered package, it can also be used independently, e.g., to generate the input for new hybrid models.

\section{Integration into \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}} \label{sec:integration}

In the implementation, each hybrid model predicts only its core property; all derived properties and phase equilibrium calculations are provided by \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}.
For HANNA, for instance, only a function returning the excess Gibbs energy $G^{\rm E}$ as a function of temperature and composition is defined.
Activity coefficients and the excess enthalpy are then obtained with Clapeyron's generic property functions, e.g., \texttt{activity\_coefficient(model,p,T,z)} and \texttt{excess(model,p,T,z,enthalpy)}, which evaluate the general thermodynamic relations
\begin{align}
\ln \gamma_i &= \frac{1}{RT} \left(\frac{\partial G^{\rm E}}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,p,n_{j\neq i}} \\
h^{\rm E} &= -RT^2 \left(\frac{\partial \left(g^{\rm E}/RT\right)}{\partial T}\right)_{p,\bm{x}}
\end{align}
by automatic differentiation.
For transport properties, no such general relations exist; to provide a consistent interface, the transport models thus instead extend the property functions of \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl}.

The models are constructed with the same syntax as in \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} and can be freely combined with its existing models, e.g., a $g^{\rm E}$ model with any pure-component model via the keyword argument \texttt{puremodel} or within a \texttt{CompositeModel} (see Section~\ref{sec:ex_process}).

\section{Examples} \label{sec:examples}
\subsection{Phase equilibria} \label{sec:ex_mix}

Code~\ref{lst:pxy} computes the isothermal $p$-$xy$ diagram of the system tetrachloromethane (1) + \textit{tert}-butanol (2) at 298.15~K.
HANNA and GRAPPA are constructed from the component names only, which are resolved to SMILES via the \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} database.
HANNA provides the activity coefficients and GRAPPA the pure-component vapor pressures, while the bubble-point calculation based on the extended Raoult's law is performed by Clapeyron's \texttt{bubble\_pressure} function.
GRAPPA could be replaced by any other pure-component model, e.g., a cubic EoS, without changing the rest of the code.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pxy}, the VLE is qualitatively well described; the slight overestimation of the pressure results from slightly overestimated pure-component vapor pressures.

\subsection{Chemical process simulation} \label{sec:ex_process}

As a more complex application example, the hybrid models are coupled with \texttt{ModelingToolkit.jl} \cite{ma_modelingtoolkit_2021}, a framework for symbolic-numeric equation-based modeling, to simulate a batch-distillation process.
The column is described by the equilibrium-stage concept, in which each stage is modeled by the MESH equations, i.e., mass balances, phase equilibrium, summation conditions, and energy balance \cite{gorak_distillation_2014}.
The implementation is available at \href{https://github.com/se-schmitt/BatchDistillation.jl}{github.com/se-schmitt/BatchDistillation.jl}.

In the example, we simulate the separation of methyl benzoate + ethyl benzoate + dimethyl phthalate in a column with 12 theoretical stages (Code~\ref{lst:batch}).
The thermodynamic model is assembled with Clapeyron's \texttt{CompositeModel}: COGNAC provides the activity coefficients and GRAPPA the vapor pressures, while the liquid densities (DIPPR 105) and the gas phase (Tsonopoulos virial correlation) are described by models from \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}.
The reboiler is charged with 20 mol of the mixture (mole fractions 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2) and heated with a constant duty of 300~W at 10~kPa; the reflux ratio is one.
Starting from steady state at total reflux, the differential-algebraic system generated by \texttt{ModelingToolkit.jl} is integrated until the reboiler runs dry or one component is purified.

\section{Conclusions}

\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} makes state-of-the-art hybrid models combining machine learning with physical knowledge for thermophysical property prediction available in Julia, and its subpackage \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl} provides the chemical language models required as their input.
As the models are integrated into \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}, they directly benefit from its solvers and can be combined with classical models.
The examples show that this enables hybrid models for applications ranging from simple property calculations to dynamic process simulations.
\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} will be continuously extended with new hybrid models.
