\begin{abstract}

Hybrid models that combine physical knowledge with machine learning are attracting growing interest for predicting thermodynamic properties.
They often outperform classical models in accuracy while being applicable to a much wider scope of chemicals.
However, their application in engineering applications remains rare as they are often hardly accessible through a unified programmatic interface.
To close this gap, we present \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}, a Julia package that provides a variety of state-of-the-art hybrid models for thermodynamic property prediction.
\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} includes hybrid models for pure component properties like the vapor pressure, mixture properties for predicting phase equilibria, and transport properties.
All models only require the SMILES of the molecules as input which makes them applicable to nearly all chemical systems.
\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} is built on the \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} and \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} packages, leveraging their rich thermodynamic solver ecosystem. 
The \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} models significantly improve molecular property prediction in various applications in science and engineering, e.g., chemical process engineering, and can be coupled with Julia's ecosystem for scientific modeling and simulation.
This is demonstrated here for several examples.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge of thermodynamic properties of fluids is crucial in many fields of engineering and science, e.g., for developing new chemical and biotechnological processes, optimizing heat pumps, or designing carbon capture and storage technologies. 
However, experimental data on thermodynamic properties are notoriously scarce due to the high cost and effort of measurements, making reliable prediction models indispensable. 
In recent years, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a particularly promising approach to thermodynamic modeling.
Hybrid thermodynamic models combine ML with physical knowledge \cite{hasse_artificial_2026}.

Unlike purely data-driven ML models, hybrid models are thermodynamically consistent, i.e., they obey hard physical constraints.
This consistency is either achieved by an appropriate architecture \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1,hoffmann_machine-learned_2025} or by exploiting existing thermodynamic models to form new hybrid models \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1, hayer_advancing_2025,hoffmann_grappahybrid_2025}.
Based only on the SMILES of a substance \cite{weininger_smiles_1988} -- a textual representation of the molecular structure -- the hybrid models predict thermodynamic properties, outperforming established models in both accuracy and scope \cite{hoffmann_machine-learned_2025}.
Among the covered properties are phase equilibria of pure substances and mixtures \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}, which are central to chemical process design, as well as transport properties such as diffusion coefficients \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1} that govern molecular mass transfer.

Most hybrid models use a chemical representation method to encode the chemical information numerically. 
For this purpose, several techniques exist.
Frequently applied chemical representation methods are chemical language models and molecular graph neural networks (GNNs). 
Chemical language models operate on the SMILES and are pretrained on molecular properties.
They deliver a molecular embedding as output which can, without retraining, be used as input for hybrid thermodynamic models (the prediction heads).
Examples of chemical language models are ChemBERTa-2 \cite{ahmad_chemberta-2_2022} or SMI-TED \cite{soares_open-source_2025}.
A different approach is used by molecular graph neural networks \cite{wieder_compact_2020}.
Here, the molecular structure is encoded in a GNN which is then trained together with the prediction heads directly on the thermophysical properties.
This directly encodes the molecular structure in the architecture of the machine learning model.

In Julia, a rich ecosystem for thermodynamics and chemical engineering already exists. 
\texttt{Clapeyron.jl} \cite{walker_clapeyronjl_2022}, a mature and comprehensive thermodynamic package, is a central part of this ecosystem. It combines a large number of thermodynamic models (including equations of state (EoS) and excess Gibbs energy ($g^{\rm E}$) models) with advanced and efficient solvers. Leveraging Julia's excellent extensibility, several packages extend \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}, e.g., \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} \cite{schmitt_se-schmittentropyscalingjl_2025} for modeling transport properties, or \href{https://github.com/ClapeyronThermo/Langmuir.jl}{\texttt{Langmuir.jl}} \cite{santana_langmuirjl_nodate} for modeling adsorption.

This paper introduces \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}, which provides Julia implementations of hybrid thermodynamic models, based on \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} and \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} -- enabling the prediction of thermodynamic properties for any substance in Julia.
\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} complements the interactive website \url{https://ml-prop.mv.rptu.de/} \cite{hoffmann_mlprop_2025} which enables the online calculation of thermodynamic properties based on hybrid models.
Additionally, the subpackage \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl} which provides implementations of chemical language models is presented.
An overview of both packages is given in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.
Due to the excellent extensibility of Julia packages, \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} substantially broadens the capabilities for modeling chemical processes, based on state-of-the-art molecular property prediction. 
The individual models are presented and their integration into the existing thermodynamic ecosystem is explained. 
Additionally, illustrative applications of these models in chemical process simulations are showcased using packages from the wider scientific-modeling ecosystem in Julia.

\section{Package overview}

\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} implements several hybrid models and makes them accessible to the thermodynamic solvers from \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}.
This integration simplifies the implementation of new models and enables making use of the existing thermodynamic solvers already implemented in \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}. 

\subsection{Models}

\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} currently includes models for pure component properties (see section~\ref{sec:pure}), liquid mixture properties, i.e., $g^{\rm E}$ models (see section~\ref{sec:mix}), and transport properties (see section~\ref{sec:transport}).
Additionally, chemical language models are implemented in the subpackage \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl} (see section~\ref{sec:language}).
An overview of all model classes and models is given in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}.
For an up-to-date list of models, see the \href{https://se-schmitt.github.io/MLThermoProperties.jl/stable/models}{documentation}.

\subsubsection{Pure component models} \label{sec:pure}

GRAPPA is a hybrid GNN for the prediction of the vapor pressure of pure components, trained on experimental data of about 25,000 components \cite{hoffmann_grappahybrid_2025}.
A graph attention network encodes the molecular graph derived from the SMILES and a prediction head yields the parameters of the Antoine equation, from which vapor pressures and boiling points follow at any temperature.
Additionally, self-diffusion coefficients of pure components can be calculated by ESE (see section~\ref{sec:transport}).

\subsubsection{Mixture models} \label{sec:mix}

The implemented $g^{\rm E}$ models follow two hybridization strategies \cite{hasse_artificial_2026}: physical constraints are either hard-coded into the architecture of a flexible neural network, or machine learning is used to predict the parameters of an established physical model.
All of them provide activity coefficients, from which vapor-liquid equilibria (VLE) can be calculated in combination with a vapor pressure model (e.g., GRAPPA), as well as liquid-liquid equilibria (LLE) and excess properties such as the excess enthalpy $h^{\rm E}$.

HANNA follows the first strategy: it is a hard-constraint neural network that predicts the excess Gibbs energy $g^{\rm E}$ from the SMILES of the components, the temperature, and the composition \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}.
Pure-component embeddings from ChemBERTa-2 (see section~\ref{sec:language}) are processed by a permutation-invariant deep-set architecture, and the output is corrected such that the pure-component limits are fulfilled; the activity coefficients follow by automatic differentiation and thus obey the Gibbs-Duhem equation by construction.
Two versions are available: \texttt{ogHANNA}, the original model trained on activity coefficients in binary mixtures \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}, and \texttt{HANNA} (alias \texttt{multHANNA}), which is additionally trained on LLE data and excess enthalpies and extended to multi-component mixtures by a geometric projection method \cite{hoffmann_thermodynamically_2026}.
As the neural network is part of the $g^{\rm E}$ model itself, it has to be evaluated at every state point.

UNIFAC 2.0 and modified UNIFAC 2.0 follow the second strategy and retain the equations of the group-contribution methods UNIFAC and modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) and only replace their parameter tables \cite{hayer_advancing_2025,hayer_modified_2025}.
A matrix completion method predicts all pair-interaction parameters between the structural groups, including those missing in the published tables, and is trained end-to-end on experimental activity coefficients (and excess enthalpies for modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) 2.0) from the Dortmund Data Bank.
This considerably extends the applicability and improves the accuracy compared to the original versions.
Both UNIFAC models are implemented directly in \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} as this only requires new parameter tables.

COGNAC \cite{hoffmann_learning_2026} also follows the second strategy, using a GNN to predict the parameters of the local-composition framework of modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) for individual atoms instead of predefined structural groups.
A message-passing neural network encodes each atom together with its molecular neighborhood, from which two prediction heads yield atomic volume and surface-area parameters as well as interaction parameters for every atom pair.
These parameters enter the unchanged modified UNIFAC (Dortmund) equations, so that thermodynamic consistency is guaranteed and the neural network has to be evaluated only once per set of components rather than at every state point.
COGNAC was trained on about 820,000 experimental data points comprising VLE, LLE, solid-liquid equilibria (SLE), activity coefficients at infinite dilution, and excess enthalpies.

\subsubsection{Transport models} \label{sec:transport}

Both transport models are based on the Stokes-Einstein (SE) equation, whose prediction is multiplied by a positive scaling factor obtained from a neural network ensemble that takes molecular descriptors derived from the SMILES as input.
As the scaling factor does not depend on temperature, the physically consistent temperature dependence of the SE equation is retained.

ESE (Enhanced Stokes-Einstein) predicts diffusion coefficients $D_{ij}^\infty$ of a solute $i$ at infinite dilution in a pure solvent $j$ \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1}.
The only inputs are the SMILES of solute and solvent and the solvent viscosity at the temperature of interest, which can be provided by any viscosity model of \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl}.
Choosing the solute as solvent ($j=i$) yields the self-diffusion coefficient of the pure component.

HADES extends ESE to composition-dependent self-diffusion coefficients in liquid mixtures with any number of components, covering the entire range from infinite dilution to the pure components \cite{wagner_composition-dependent_2026}.
The descriptors of the solvent components are aggregated by a deep-set model, which ensures invariance with respect to their permutation, and the viscosity of the mixed solvent is obtained by logarithmic mixing of the pure-component viscosities.
HADES was trained on 2,526 experimental data points for 600 systems; besides the SMILES, the pure-component viscosities are required as input.

\subsubsection{Chemical language models} \label{sec:language}

In \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl}, two language models are available: ChemBERTa-2 \cite{ahmad_chemberta-2_2022} and SMI-TED \cite{soares_open-source_2025}.
ChemBERTa-2 is a BERT-type transformer that was pretrained on the SMILES of 77 million molecules from PubChem by regression of molecular descriptors \cite{ahmad_chemberta-2_2022}.
\texttt{ChemBERTa.jl} provides the variant used in HANNA, whose tokenizer was fixed by Specht et al. \cite{specht_hanna_2024-1}.
Before tokenization, the SMILES are canonicalized with RDKit, either via \texttt{RDKitMinimalLib.jl} or via \texttt{PythonCall.jl}.
SMI-TED is a transformer-based encoder-decoder foundation model with 289 million parameters that was pretrained on 91 million curated SMILES from PubChem by masked token prediction and reconstruction of the SMILES from its latent space \cite{soares_open-source_2025}.
The encoder condenses the token embeddings into a fixed-size latent representation of the molecule, which, owing to the reconstruction objective, separates molecules according to chemically relevant features and serves as molecular embedding.
The decoder, a non-autoregressive feed-forward neural network, can be used to generate a SMILES from a given embedding.
Both models are implemented natively in Julia based on \texttt{Lux.jl} \cite{pal_lux_2026} in the registered subpackage \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl}, which can also be used independently, e.g., as molecular input representation for new hybrid models.

\subsection{Integration in Julia's thermodynamic ecosystem}

All models are implemented by extending a property function from \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} or \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} and making use of existing thermodynamic solvers.
This is exemplarily explained for the \texttt{HANNA} model and the \texttt{ESE} model in the following.
For the $g^{\rm E}$ model \texttt{HANNA}, a custom function is defined which calculates the total excess Gibbs energy $G^{\rm E}$ through a neural network (see Code~\ref{lst:integration_hanna}).
This is the only thermodynamic property that needs to be defined.
Derived quantities like the activity coefficients $\gamma_i$ and the excess enthalpy $h^{\rm E}$ can be calculated based on the HANNA model by Clapeyron's property functions, i.e., \texttt{Clapeyron.activity\_coefficient(model::HANNA,p,T,z)} and \texttt{Clapeyron.excess(model::HANNA,p,T,z,enthalpy)}, without further ado.
This exploits the universal thermodynamic relations between those properties which can be calculated as derivatives of the excess Gibbs energy, i.e.,

\begin{align}
\ln \gamma_i &= \frac{1}{RT} \left(\frac{\partial G^{\rm E}}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,p,n_{j\neq i}} \\
h^{\rm E} &= -RT^2 \left(\frac{\partial \left(g^{\rm E}/RT\right)}{\partial T}\right)_{p,\bm{x}}.
\end{align}

These universal thermodynamic relations are already implemented in \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}. 
The required derivatives are evaluated by automatic differentiation.

The \texttt{ESE} model follows the same principle, but extends \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} instead.
The only required thermodynamic method implemented evaluates the Stokes-Einstein equation corrected by a factor $b_{ij}$, which a neural network ensemble predicts from molecular descriptors of solute $i$ and solvent $j$ \cite{wagner_hybrid_2026-1} (see Code~\ref{lst:integration_ese}), i.e.,

\begin{equation}
D_{ij}^\infty = b_{ij}\,\frac{k_\mathrm{B}T}{6\pi\,\eta_j(p,T)\,r_i}
\end{equation}

with the Boltzmann constant $k_\mathrm{B}$ and the hydrodynamic radius $r_i$ of the solute.
Since $b_{ij}$ depends on the components only, it is evaluated once when the model is constructed and the temperature dependence is dictated entirely by the Stokes-Einstein equation, which enforces physical consistency.
The solvent viscosity $\eta_j$ is obtained by calling \texttt{EntropyScaling.viscosity(model,p,T)} on a viscosity model supplied by the user, so that any model of \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} can be combined with \texttt{ESE}, e.g., a substance-specific reference correlation (\texttt{RefpropRES}), a group-contribution entropy scaling model (\texttt{GCES}), or a constant experimental viscosity (\texttt{ConstantModel}).
Based on this single method, \texttt{EntropyScaling.inf\_diffusion\_coefficient} is readily available for \texttt{ESE}, returning the diffusion coefficients of all component pairs or of an individual solute-solvent pair.

The syntax to construct the models follows \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}'s and \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl}'s syntax closely.
Existing models from both packages can be used in combination with the new hybrid models, e.g., the $g^{\rm E}$ models can be used with any pure component model (defined through the keyword argument \texttt{puremodel}), and in ESE a viscosity model can be used that directly enables the prediction of the temperature dependence.

\section{Example applications}

\subsection{Calculating mixture properties}

The following example computes the isothermal $p$-$xy$ diagram of the system tetrachloromethane (1) + \textit{tert}-butanol (2) at 298.15~K with Clapeyron's \texttt{bubble\_pressure} function, using \texttt{HANNA} and \texttt{GRAPPA} (see Code~\ref{lst:pxy}).
Both models are constructed from the component names only, which are resolved to SMILES through the \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} database.
The activity coefficients $\gamma_i$ are supplied by HANNA and the vapor pressures of the pure components $p_i^{\mathrm{s}}$ by GRAPPA, while the phase-equilibrium solver, based on the extended Raoult's law, is taken from \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}.
Since the pure-component model is a free choice, GRAPPA can be replaced by a cubic or multiparameter EoS without changing the rest of the code.

The resulting phase diagram is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pxy}.
The comparison with experimental data shows a good qualitative description of the VLE.
Both pure-component vapor pressures are slightly overestimated, which results in a slight overestimation of the VLE pressure.

\subsection{Diffusion coefficients}

Transport properties are accessed through \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl}.
Code~\ref{lst:ese} computes both diffusion coefficients at infinite dilution in the binary system \textit{n}-hexane (1) + benzene (2) between 210~K and 340~K for $D_{21}^\infty$ and between 278.6~K (freezing point of benzene \cite{linstrom_nist_1997}) and 340~K for $D_{12}^\infty$.
The \texttt{ESE} model is built from the component names alone.
The solvent viscosity required by the Stokes-Einstein equation is calculated from the \texttt{ESFramework} model \cite{schmitt_entropy_2024} in combination with the SAFT-VR Mie EoS \cite{lafitte_accurate_2013}.
Alternatively, other \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl} models, e.g., group-contribution models or simply a constant viscosity, can be used by passing them via the \texttt{vismodel} keyword.
The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:diff}.
The model prediction agrees very well with the experimental data.
The temperature dependence, which results from both the Stokes-Einstein equation and the viscosity prediction, is very well captured.

\subsection{Chemical process simulation}

The hybrid models implemented in \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} can easily be coupled with Julia's modeling and simulation ecosystem.
This is particularly interesting for, e.g., applications in chemical engineering, where access to thermodynamic models is crucial.
An important task in chemical engineering is the modeling and design of batch distillation processes to separate a given mixture.
Batch distillation columns can be modeled by the equilibrium stage concept, where every stage is described by the MESH equations \cite{gorak_distillation_2014}, which include the mass balances (M), phase equilibrium (E), the summation condition (S), and the energy balance (H for enthalpy).
A Julia implementation of batch distillation simulations based on the MESH equations is given in \href{https://github.com/se-schmitt/BatchDistillation.jl}{github.com/se-schmitt/BatchDistillation.jl}.
The implementation is based on \texttt{ModelingToolkit.jl} \cite{ma_modelingtoolkit_2021}, a framework for symbolic-numeric equation-based modeling.
\texttt{ModelingToolkit.jl} is widely applied for acausal modeling tasks.

As an example, the separation of the mixture methyl benzoate + ethyl benzoate + dimethyl phthalate in a batch distillation column with 12 (theoretical) stages is simulated (see Code~\ref{lst:batch}).
The thermodynamic model is assembled with \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}'s \texttt{CompositeModel}: COGNAC provides the activity coefficients, GRAPPA the vapor pressures (both from \texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl}), the DIPPR 105 correlation the liquid densities, and the Tsonopoulos virial correlation the gas phase (both natively from \texttt{Clapeyron.jl}).
The reboiler is charged with 20 mol of the mixture ($x_i = \{ 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 \}$) and heated with a constant duty of 300 W at a pressure of 10 kPa.
Half of the condensed vapor is withdrawn as distillate; the rest is returned to the column as reflux.
Starting from steady state at total reflux, the differential-algebraic system generated by \texttt{ModelingToolkit.jl} is integrated until the reboiler runs dry or one component is purified.

The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:batch}.

The right part of the figure shows the temperature profiles in the column at several times.
Due to the advancing removal of the lower-boiling components, the temperature increases over time in all stages, from about 400~K to 470~K.
While all stages have a similar temperature at the beginning and at the end of the simulation, a steep temperature front moves through the column in between as ethyl benzoate is depleted.

\section{Conclusions}

\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} makes hybrid thermodynamic models that combine machine learning with physical knowledge available in Julia.
By implementing each model as an extension of a single property function of \texttt{Clapeyron.jl} or \texttt{EntropyScaling.jl}, the models inherit the complete solver ecosystem of these packages: e.g., activity coefficients, excess properties, and phase equilibria follow from one machine-learned expression for the excess Gibbs energy, and derived properties are obtained by automatic differentiation without analytical derivatives.
This simplifies the calculation of thermophysical properties with these hybrid models.
Additionally, it enables the application of the hybrid models in complex simulations, e.g., for applications in chemical engineering.

Moreover, the subpackage \texttt{ChemBERTa.jl} provides implementations of chemical language models, in particular ChemBERTa-2 and SMI-TED.
These models can be used beneficially in many areas of cheminformatics to embed the chemical information of a molecule based only on its SMILES.

The package is constantly extended by new hybrid models.
The models implemented here are also accessible through the MLPROP web interface \cite{hoffmann_mlprop_2025}, which is aimed at quick interactive calculations of single systems.
\texttt{MLThermoProperties.jl} complements this by making the same models available inside automated workflows: they can be called in loops, embedded in process models, and combined with the wider Julia ecosystem for scientific modeling and simulation, as demonstrated above.
